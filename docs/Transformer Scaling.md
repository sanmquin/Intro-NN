# Transformer Scaling

How do architectural degrees of freedom constrain the class of sequence transformations the model can represent?

The first discipline is tensor analysis. A transformer is fundamentally a sequence of tensor contractions. The hidden dimension, the number of heads, and the sequence length define the shape of these tensors. Increasing the hidden dimension increases the dimensionality of the vector space in which token representations live. Increasing the number of heads does not simply increase dimensionality; instead, it decomposes the representation into multiple lower-dimensional projections. The central question is therefore not “how many heads?” but rather “what tensor decompositions become representable?” The appropriate mathematical tools are tensor rank, CP decomposition, Tucker decomposition, and tensor networks. Good references are Kolda & Bader’s Tensor Decompositions and Applications and Cichocki’s Tensor Networks for Dimensionality Reduction and Large-scale Optimization. These provide a language for reasoning about how architectural dimensions affect representational capacity.

The second discipline is operator theory, because every transformer layer defines a linear (or locally linear) operator acting on a product space consisting of token positions and feature dimensions. The hidden dimension determines the codomain of the operator, while the sequence length determines its domain. When the sequence grows, the operator itself changes size. The interesting question becomes whether there exists a limiting operator as the sequence length tends to infinity. This leads naturally to compact operators, integral kernels, spectral theory, and operator convergence. Functional analysis texts such as Conway’s A Course in Functional Analysis or Reed & Simon become directly relevant here. This perspective lets you ask whether doubling the hidden dimension changes the spectrum of the operator, or merely refines its approximation.

A third direction comes from approximation theory, which asks how expressive a transformer becomes as its architectural parameters increase. Here the hidden dimension resembles the width of a neural network, the number of layers resembles its compositional depth, and the sequence length resembles the dimensionality of the input domain. Approximation theory provides upper and lower bounds on what classes of functions become representable as these quantities grow. The literature on Barron spaces, neural approximation theory, and more recently transformer approximation theorems belongs here. Papers by Yarotsky, Petersen & Voigtlaender, and more recent transformer approximation results by Yun et al. are representative. This line of work is one of the few that attempts to relate architectural parameters to representational power rather than empirical performance.

The fourth perspective is information theory, because each architectural parameter imposes a bottleneck on information flow. The embedding dimension limits how much information can be encoded in each token representation. The number of heads limits how many independent attention patterns can coexist simultaneously. The sequence length determines the amount of information entering the computation. Vocabulary size affects the entropy of the input distribution and therefore the complexity of the embedding space. Rather than studying dimensions geometrically, one studies channel capacity, mutual information, redundancy, and compression. Cover & Thomas’s Elements of Information Theory provides the mathematical foundation, while work by Tishby and collaborators introduces the Information Bottleneck perspective for neural networks.

The fifth discipline is random matrix theory, which becomes increasingly useful as architectural dimensions become large. Modern transformers operate in regimes where hidden dimensions are several thousand, attention matrices may be several thousand by several thousand, and weight matrices contain millions of parameters. Rather than analyzing individual matrices, one studies the statistical behavior of their spectra. Questions such as “How many heads should a transformer have?” can be reformulated as “At what point does the spectrum of the attention operators cease to gain additional independent directions?” Random matrix theory provides tools to distinguish genuine increases in representational complexity from redundant parameter growth. Tao’s Topics in Random Matrix Theory, Mehta’s Random Matrices, and Couillet & Debbah’s Random Matrix Methods for Wireless Communications are particularly valuable because they emphasize asymptotic spectral behavior.

Another essential perspective is computational complexity. Here one asks not whether an architecture can represent a function, but what computational resources are required. Hidden dimension, depth, vocabulary size, and sequence length all become computational resources analogous to time and memory in classical algorithms. Recent theoretical work by Merrill, Hahn, Chiang, and others studies transformers from the perspective of automata theory and circuit complexity, asking which formal languages become recognizable as these architectural parameters increase. This provides one of the few rigorous frameworks relating architectural scaling to computational capability.

Finally—and I think this is the direction closest to your recent line of thought—there is dimensional analysis of architectural scaling, where every architectural hyperparameter is treated as an independent degree of freedom in a coupled dynamical system. Instead of studying hidden dimension, heads, layers, vocabulary, or sequence length independently, you define dimensionless ratios such as

* hidden dimension divided by vocabulary entropy,
* attention rank divided by sequence length,
* head dimension divided by intrinsic embedding dimension,
* effective operator rank divided by layer depth.

The goal is to identify invariant quantities that predict behavior across architectures. This is analogous to how fluid mechanics replaces raw variables with Reynolds, Mach, or Prandtl numbers. Remarkably, transformer theory has almost no equivalent. Most empirical scaling laws, such as those by Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. (“Chinchilla”), fit power laws relating model size, data, and compute to loss. They do not explain why those exponents arise, nor do they relate architectural quantities through invariant combinations.

This is where I think there is a significant theoretical gap. The current literature is rich in empirical scaling laws and expressive-power theorems, but it lacks a unified analytical framework that explains how hidden dimension, head count, layer depth, sequence length, and vocabulary size jointly determine the geometry of the computation. Building such a framework would likely require combining tensor analysis, operator theory, approximation theory, information theory, and asymptotic spectral analysis into a single mathematical description of transformers. Based on the questions you’ve been asking over the past several days, that seems much closer to the theory you’re trying to develop than the current interpretability literature.
