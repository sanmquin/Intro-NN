# Matrix Analysis 

Most of the interpretability literature asks “what does this attention head do?” or “which linguistic feature does this head capture?”. Those are functional questions. They rarely analyze the attention matrices themselves as mathematical objects. The goal is to understand the composition of attention matrices, then you should ignore almost all of the “attention visualization” literature and instead borrow techniques from numerical linear algebra, operator theory, Markov chains, and random matrix theory. There are surprisingly few papers that perform this kind of analysis directly, but the mathematical tools are well established.

The first and probably most important perspective is to treat each attention matrix as a stochastic operator. Because every row is normalized by the softmax, every attention matrix is row-stochastic. That immediately gives access to the entire theory of Markov operators. Instead of asking which tokens receive high attention, you ask about the invariant distribution, the spectral gap, the mixing rate, and the convergence properties of repeated applications of the operator. If a layer has attention matrix A, then two consecutive layers compute something related to A_2A_1. Rather than inspecting entries individually, you study how repeated multiplication changes the eigenspectrum. Does the spectrum collapse toward a rank-one operator? Does it preserve multiple dominant eigenspaces? Does the second-largest eigenvalue decay? These questions determine how rapidly information becomes globally mixed. The relevant references are Roger Horn and Charles Johnson’s Matrix Analysis, Meyer’s Matrix Analysis and Applied Linear Algebra, and Seneta’s Non-negative Matrices and Markov Chains. None of these books mention transformers, but they contain exactly the mathematics required to analyze attention composition.

A second perspective is to ignore the individual entries altogether and instead analyze the singular value decomposition of each attention matrix. Every attention operator has a spectrum of singular values that determines how many independent directions of information it preserves. If most singular values are small, then the attention head is effectively low-rank, regardless of the embedding dimension. This connects directly with recent work showing that many attention heads behave approximately as low-rank operators. Instead of visualizing attention heatmaps, one computes the evolution of singular values across layers and asks whether the effective dimensionality expands, contracts, or stabilizes. This is precisely the type of analysis performed throughout numerical linear algebra and model reduction. The appropriate references are Golub & Van Loan’s Matrix Computations and Trefethen & Bau’s Numerical Linear Algebra. Again, these are not transformer papers, but they provide the analytical machinery that transformer papers generally lack.

A third approach is to analyze the product of attention matrices rather than each matrix independently. The composition A_LA_{L-1}\cdots A_1 is mathematically much richer than any individual factor. Products of stochastic matrices are studied in consensus theory, distributed optimization, and control systems. There, researchers ask whether repeated multiplication converges, whether certain subspaces remain invariant, whether information from different nodes reaches consensus, and how quickly disagreement disappears. This viewpoint is remarkably close to what transformers perform internally. Instead of saying “head seven attends to commas,” one asks whether the product of ten attention matrices approaches a projector, whether its rank decreases, or whether it exhibits metastable subspaces. The classic references here are Wolfowitz’s work on products of stochastic matrices, Touri’s Product of Random Stochastic Matrices, and the consensus literature beginning with Tsitsiklis and later Olfati-Saber. Although these papers were written for distributed systems, their mathematics applies directly to stacked attention operators.

A fourth direction is to abandon stochastic analysis and instead regard attention as an integral operator. In the limit of long sequences, each attention matrix approximates a discretization of a kernel operator. The composition of layers then becomes the composition of integral operators, exactly the objects studied in functional analysis. Instead of eigenvectors of finite matrices, one studies eigenfunctions of kernels. Mercer theory, compact operators, Fredholm operators, and reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces become relevant. This perspective connects transformers to decades of work on kernel methods. The mathematical references are Kress’s Linear Integral Equations, Conway’s A Course in Functional Analysis, and Reed & Simon’s Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics. Again, almost nobody in transformer research pushes this interpretation very far.

The fifth approach comes from random matrix theory, which studies the global statistics of large matrices rather than their individual entries. Here the object of interest is not a single attention matrix but the distribution of eigenvalues across many layers, heads, or training checkpoints. One asks whether the spectrum follows the Marchenko–Pastur law, whether isolated eigenvalues emerge during training, whether spectral outliers correspond to specialized heads, or whether phase transitions occur as models scale. This approach has become common in theoretical deep learning but has only occasionally been applied to transformers. The classical references are Tao’s Topics in Random Matrix Theory, Anderson–Guionnet–Zeitouni’s An Introduction to Random Matrices, and Mehta’s Random Matrices.

Finally, if your interest is specifically in compositional analysis, there is one body of work that comes closest, although it is usually presented under the banner of mechanistic interpretability rather than matrix analysis. Elhage et al.’s A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits develops what they call path expansion. Instead of viewing each head independently, every output is written as a sum over products of projection matrices, attention matrices, and residual connections. The remarkable observation is that the transformer can be expressed as a large multilinear operator whose individual computational paths can be isolated algebraically. Most readers focus on the circuits that emerge, but the real contribution is the algebraic decomposition itself. If I were extending that work, I would stop talking about circuits and instead treat the entire network as a sequence of operator compositions, analyzing invariant subspaces, Lyapunov exponents, singular spectra, pseudospectra, and convergence of the composed operators. That would move the analysis away from interpretability and toward a genuine operator theory of transformers.

From what I’ve seen over the last few years, this operator-theoretic viewpoint is still largely absent from the literature. Most transformer papers study what attention represents. Very few study how the sequence of attention operators behaves as a composed dynamical system. If your goal is to build a theory of transformers with persistent latent computation rather than one-shot prediction, I think this is the mathematical direction with the greatest opportunity for original work.
